1

Review: ser, estar, tener, gustar

10 min

These four verbs, together, let you talk about identity, location, possession and preference — almost everything you've practised across the course. Quick refresh before we move to accents.

SER

identity — permanent

  • Soy agente de viajes.
  • Eres muy simpática.
  • Es de Sevilla.

ESTAR

location / temporary state

  • Estoy en Sevilla.
  • Estás muy contenta hoy.
  • La Feria está cerca del río.

TENER

age / possession

  • Tengo treinta y dos años.
  • ¿Tienes un traje de flamenco?
  • Sofía tiene mucha familia aquí.

GUSTAR

likes / preferences

  • Me gusta el flamenco.
  • Te gustan las tapas.
  • Nos gusta la Feria de Abril.
The one rule that ties them together: ser/tener/gustar describe things that are stable (who you are, what you have, what you like) — estar is the odd one out, always pointing at "right now" (where you are, how you feel today).
2

Sounding like Sevilla

10 min

Andalusian Spanish doesn't change the grammar you've learned — it changes how it sounds. Here are the three features you'll hear most with Sofía's family and friends.

1 · Dropped final -s

The "-s" at the end of words (plurals, "tú" verb forms) often disappears or becomes a soft breath.

"¿Cómo estás?" → "¿Cómo etá?"

2 · Dropped -d- and final -d

The "d" between vowels (especially in -ado/-ada endings) is often dropped completely.

"pescado" → "pescao"

3 · Softer, faster consonants

Overall, speech is quicker and more relaxed than the "textbook" Spanish you've been trained on — words blend together more.

"para nada" → "pa' na'"
Don't panic: you are not expected to speak like this — only to recognise it. Sofía's family will understand your "textbook" Spanish perfectly, and will likely slow down for you once they know you're learning. This is about training your ear, not changing how you talk.
